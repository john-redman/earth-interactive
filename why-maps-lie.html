Why maps lie about the size of countries

By John Redman · Updated 5 October 2026

On the map you grew up with, Greenland looks about as big as Africa. Africa is really about fourteen times larger. Here is why, and how to see the world at its true size.

You can't flatten an orange peel

The Earth is (very nearly) a sphere. Paper and screens are flat. Every world map has to stretch, squash or tear the surface to make it lie flat, and no map can keep everything right at once. Mapmakers choose what to preserve: areas, shapes, distances or directions. Whatever they choose, something else gets distorted. The way a map does this is called its projection.

Mercator: made for sailors

In 1569 the Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator published a world map for navigators. Its trick: any straight line on the map is a line of constant compass bearing. A sailor could draw a line from port to port, read off the angle and steer that course. To make this work, Mercator kept the shape of small areas correct everywhere. The price was size.

On a globe, the lines of longitude meet at the poles. On Mercator's map they are drawn as parallel lines, so everything far from the equator gets stretched sideways. To keep shapes right, the map then stretches those places north–south by the same amount. The result: the further from the equator, the bigger things look.

How big is the stretch?

On a Mercator map, an area at latitude φ is enlarged by 1 / cos²φ. In plain numbers:

LatitudeDrawn this much too bigFor example
0°1× (true size)Ecuador, DR Congo, Indonesia
30°1.3×Egypt, southern United States
45°2×Northern Italy, Oregon
55°3×United Kingdom, Moscow
60°4×Oslo, Helsinki, southern Alaska
70°8.5×Northern Norway, much of Greenland
80°33×Northern Greenland, Svalbard
90°InfiniteThe poles can't be shown at all

That last row is why most Mercator maps simply stop at about 85° north and south, and why Antarctica appears as an endless white strip along the bottom, if it appears at all.

Famous illusions

  • Greenland and Africa. Greenland covers about 2.17 million km². Africa covers about 30.4 million km², fourteen times as much. On Mercator they look similar. Even DR Congo alone (2.34 million km²) is bigger than Greenland, and so is Algeria.
  • Greenland and India. India is about 1.5 times the size of Greenland, yet on most maps Greenland looks several times bigger. Greenland vs India in numbers, or compare them on the globe.
  • Russia and Africa. Russia is the largest country on Earth at 17.1 million km², but Africa is still almost twice its size. On Mercator, Russia looks larger.
  • Australia. Close to the equator, Australia looks modest on flat maps. At 7.7 million km² it is almost as big as the contiguous United States. Australia vs the United States.
  • The United Kingdom. At around 54° north the UK is drawn about three times too big. Put it next to the United States or Nigeria and it shrinks.
  • Scandinavia. Norway, Sweden and Finland look huge on wall maps. Together they are smaller than DR Congo.

Why is Mercator still everywhere?

Partly habit, and partly because it is genuinely useful. Web maps such as Google Maps, Apple Maps and OpenStreetMap use a version called Web Mercator. When you zoom into a city, it keeps streets at right angles and buildings in their real shape, which matters far more than the size of Greenland. The trouble starts when the same projection is used to show the whole world, especially on classroom walls.

Better flat maps exist

Equal-area projections such as Equal Earth, Mollweide or Gall–Peters keep sizes honest, at the cost of bending shapes near the edges. Compromise projections such as Robinson or Winkel tripel balance both. National Geographic switched its world maps from Robinson to Winkel tripel in 1998 for exactly this reason. None of them is perfect, because no flat map can be.

The only honest map is a globe

A globe keeps areas, shapes, distances and directions right all at once. That is why EarthInteractive is a 3D globe. When you compare two countries, both are lifted off the surface at their true size and can be dragged anywhere, so you can lay the United Kingdom over the United States, or slide Greenland down to the equator and watch it stay the same size.
